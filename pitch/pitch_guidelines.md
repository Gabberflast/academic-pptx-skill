# Pitch Deck Content Guidelines

For fundraising decks: pre-seed, seed and Series A, accelerator applications, demo
days. The structure follows what investors themselves publish, chiefly Y Combinator's
seed deck template and design advice, Sequoia's business plan outline and a16z's
advice to its accelerator founders (links at the end).

A pitch deck is the same kind of object as an academic talk: an argument made to people
who evaluate arguments for a living. The claim is "this company will be very large and
this team will build it", and each slide is one step of the case.

---

## 1. What the deck is for

The deck does not close the round. It earns the next conversation. An investor going
through a stack of decks gives each a quick first pass, so every slide has to land its
point at a glance, and the deck as a whole should leave five to seven sentences in the
reader's head. Those sentences are your slide titles.

YC's design advice reduces to three words: slides should be **legible, simple and
obvious**. Large type with strong contrast; one idea per slide; a point that a stranger
would restate correctly after a glance.

## 2. Two formats: ask which

| | Read-ahead deck | Presented deck |
|---|-----------------|----------------|
| Used | Emailed before or after a meeting; most decks | Demo day, a stage, a partner meeting on a screen |
| Must | Stand alone with no narrator | Support a speaker; the room is listening, not reading |
| Words | Up to about 25 per slide plus a source line | Fewer: a title and one number or picture |
| Type | 24 pt body | As large as fits |
| Length | 10–12 slides plus appendix | The same sections, sometimes one slide for two |

Default to the read-ahead deck when the user does not say; it is the one that gets
forwarded. The kit's pitch mode is tuned for it. For a stage deck, use more `hero`
slides and fewer supporting points.

## 3. Intake: the facts to collect first

Ask the founder for these before outlining. Do not fill gaps with plausible numbers;
use `NEEDS("…")` and list what is missing when you hand over. The general intake round
(input material, colour scheme, slot, house rules) is in
[../customization.md](../customization.md); this list is the pitch-specific content.

- **One line**: what the company does, in words a stranger would understand.
- **Problem**: who has it, how they cope today, what it costs them (time, money, risk). Any evidence: interviews, survey, their own experience.
- **Product**: what it does in three steps or fewer. Stage: idea, prototype, live.
- **Traction**: the headline metric with unit and time window (revenue, users, usage, pilots, waitlist, letters of intent), its history by month, and where the data comes from.
- **Why now**: what changed recently (technology, regulation, behaviour, cost) that makes this possible or urgent.
- **Market**: who the buyer is, how many there are, what each would pay per year.
- **Business model**: who pays, how much, how often; margins if known.
- **Competition**: what customers use instead today, including doing nothing.
- **Team**: founders only; for each, the one or two facts that make them credible for this problem, with numbers.
- **The ask**: amount, what it funds, and the milestone it reaches in 12–18 months.
- **Stage and audience**: pre-seed, seed or Series A; which investors; read-ahead or presented.
- **Brand**: one colour and a typeface, if they have them.

## 4. The sections

One slide per section is the target. YC's template treats each section after the title
as a set that is ideally one slide and rarely more than three. Ten to twelve slides
before the appendix is normal; past fifteen, the deck is showing that the founder
cannot prioritise.

Each slide carries a small section label above its title (PROBLEM, TRACTION…) so an
investor flipping back can find the slide they want, and an action title stating the
point.

| Section | The question it answers | Action title example | Exhibit | Usual failure |
|---------|-------------------------|----------------------|---------|---------------|
| **Title** | What is this company? | (company name + one line) | — | A tagline instead of a description |
| **Problem** | Who hurts, and how much? | Independent clinics lose two days a week to insurance approvals | One number, or one real customer's situation | A market trend instead of a person's problem |
| **Solution** | What do you do about it? | Ledgerline files and chases every approval automatically | Three steps, or one simple diagram | A feature list; screenshots nobody can read |
| **Traction** | Is it working? | Revenue has grown 22% a month for eight months | One chart, latest value called out | Vanity metrics; growth with no time window; cumulative charts that hide a stall |
| **Why now / insight** | Why has nobody done this, and why can you? | A 2026 rule makes insurers accept electronic approvals for the first time | One or two points | "AI is here" with nothing specific |
| **Market** | How big can this get? | 38,000 independent clinics could pay us $4.2B a year | Buyers × price arithmetic | A top-down industry figure with "if we get just 1%" |
| **Business model** | How do you make money? | Clinics pay per clinician, and serving them costs a fifth of that | Price, margin, payback | "We'll figure out monetisation later" presented as a plan |
| **Competition** | What do customers use instead, and why do you win? | Billing firms are slow; clearinghouses stop at filing | Small comparison table | "No competitors"; a 2×2 with yourself alone in the corner; a grid of ticks where you win every row |
| **Team** | Why you? | We ran clinic billing and built insurer systems before starting Ledgerline | Founders with one quantified proof each | Job titles and logos with no achievements; advisors as padding |
| **The ask** | What do you need, and what does it buy? | $3M takes us to 400 clinics and $3M in annual revenue | Amount + two or three milestones | No amount; valuation and terms on the slide; a pie chart of spend with no outcome |

Optional, when they earn their place: **Vision** (what the company is in five years if
this works; expected at Series A and by Sequoia's outline), **Product** as a separate
slide from Solution when the product is live and shows something a description cannot,
**Go-to-market** when distribution is the hard part.

### Order

The default is the order above: it is YC's seed order with "why now" and competition
added from Sequoia's outline. Two adjustments are worth making:

- **Lead with your strongest card.** Exceptional traction goes straight after the
  solution (as above) or even before it. An unusually credible team for this exact
  problem moves up to slide two or three. Weak or absent traction means the insight
  slide does that job instead.
- **Sequoia order** (purpose, problem, solution, why now, market, competition, business
  model, team, financials, vision) suits a later-stage or more analytical audience.

Whatever the order, the ask is last and stays on screen.

## 5. What changes by stage

| | Pre-seed | Seed | Series A |
|---|----------|------|----------|
| The bet | Team and insight | Early proof that people want it | A repeatable way to grow |
| Traction slide | Prototype, pilots, waitlist, interviews. Say plainly what exists | Revenue or usage by month, retention | Growth, retention by cohort, unit economics, sales efficiency |
| Team slide | Carries the most weight | Still central | Add key hires |
| Market | Bottom-up estimate | Bottom-up, with the wedge and the expansion | Same, with evidence of the expansion starting |
| Ask | Amount and the proof point it buys | Amount and the Series A milestone | Amount and the plan it funds |
| Appendix | Short | Retention, pipeline, unit economics | Cohorts, financial model summary, hiring plan |

Do not dress a pre-seed company as a seed company. Investors know what stage they are
looking at, and a slide that says "three paid pilots, started in May" is stronger than
a chart built to look like more.

## 6. Rules for every slide

**Action titles.** The title is the sentence you want remembered. "Traction" is a
label; "Revenue has grown 22% a month for eight months" is a title. Keep it to about 14
words so it stays large.

**One idea.** If a slide needs "and also", it is two slides or one slide and an
appendix slide.

**Numbers carry units, a time window and a source.** "40% growth" is noise; "40% month
over month since March (company billing data)" is evidence. Use real figures even when
they are small.

**Facts over adjectives.** Replace "revolutionary", "seamless", "world-class" and
"massive" with the number each stands in for. The linter lists them.

**Size the market bottom-up.** Number of buyers × what each pays per year, with both
factors on the slide. Add the larger adjacent market as one line of upside if you like;
do not lead with it.

**Be straight about competition.** Name the alternatives customers really use,
including the spreadsheet and doing nothing. Concede where others are stronger. A table
in which you win every row reads as a table you made up.

**Quantify the team.** One or two proof lines per founder, each with a number: what
they built, sold, shipped, published or ran. a16z's advice to founders is blunt on this:
brag, and be specific. Relevant credentials count even when they are not startup
experience. Leave advisors off.

**Show where it goes, not only where it is.** A product slide that only lists today's
features and a forecast that only extends last year both undersell. Say what the wedge
is, why it is narrow on purpose, and what it opens up. Label forecasts as forecasts.

**The ask has no terms.** Amount, use, milestone. YC's template says to leave
valuation, cap and other terms off the slide; they belong in the conversation.

**Avoid screenshots.** Interface text is too small and a screen does several things at
once. Describe what the product does in three steps, and offer a live demo. If a
picture of the product is essential, crop to one element and enlarge it.

**Charts state their own conclusion.** The title says what the chart shows; the latest
or most important value is highlighted and labelled. Do not smooth a bumpy curve.

## 7. Integrity

The same rule as for citations in an academic deck, with higher stakes: these slides
are used to raise money.

- Never invent or round up traction, revenue, user counts, growth rates, retention,
  pipeline, customer names, logos, testimonials, partnerships, credentials or market
  figures. Not for a draft, not as a placeholder that looks real.
- A pilot is not a customer; a signed letter of intent is not revenue; a waitlist
  signup is not a user. Call each what it is.
- Customer and partner logos appear only when the founder confirms the relationship
  and that they may show it.
- Third-party market numbers get a source line naming the report and year.
- Forecasts are labelled as forecasts, and sit after the actuals, in a different shade.

When something is missing, write `NEEDS("monthly revenue, Jul–Feb")` and move on. The
deck will not pass the linter until the founder fills it in, which is the point.

## 8. Design

- One brand colour, used for the number or bar the slide is about. Everything else is
  near-black on white or grey.
- One typeface. Large: 28–32 pt titles, 24 pt body.
- White background. A dark title slide is fine.
- Text toward the top of the slide; the title is the first thing read.
- No stock photos, icon rows, gradients, animations or transitions.
- A logo on the title slide is enough; it does not need to be on every slide.
- Slide numbers on every slide, so "go back to seven" works in a meeting.

Pass the brand to the kit: `createDeck({ mode: "pitch", colors: { accent: "0F766E" }, face: "Inter" })`,
or pick a named scheme (`ink`, `emerald`, `violet`, `ember`, `slate`, `midnight`). Save
the choice to the founder's `deck_rules.md` so the next deck matches.

## 9. Appendix

Slides you do not present but will be asked for. Each still has an action title.

- Retention or cohort chart
- Unit economics: price, cost to serve, cost to acquire, payback
- Pipeline or waitlist detail
- Market sizing arithmetic in full
- Product roadmap for the next 12 months
- Summary of the financial plan
- Detail on a technical claim or a regulatory point

## 10. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Section labels as titles ("Problem", "Team") | Keep the label small above the title; make the title a claim |
| Twenty-five slides | One slide per section; the rest is appendix |
| Problem slide about a trend | Make it about a person or business and what the problem costs them |
| Traction with no number | Show the number, unit and window, or do not call it traction |
| "Up 40%" | Up 40% over what period, from what base? |
| TAM from an analyst report | Buyers × annual price, both shown |
| "We have no competitors" | Name what customers use today |
| Team slide of headshots and titles | One quantified proof line per founder |
| Valuation on the ask slide | Amount, use and milestone only |
| "Thank you" as the last slide | End on the ask |
| A wall of product screenshots | Three steps in words; demo live |
| Numbers that differ from the data room | Every figure comes from the founder's own records, with the window stated |
